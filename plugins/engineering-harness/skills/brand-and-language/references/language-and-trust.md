# Language and trust

UX establishes what a user must understand; this owner resolves how the product
names and expresses it. Read the interaction, not isolated strings. Keep one name
for one concept across navigation, action, progress, result and recovery. Distinguish
necessary audience terminology from internal mechanisms that obscure the task.

An action should predict its consequence. An error should explain what failed,
what is retained, and the available next step when known. Empty, unknown, waiting,
stale and failed are different facts. An estimate is not a saved record; a proposal
is not an applied change. Do not imply a cause, completion, fix or response time
the system cannot establish. Keep technical detail available where operators need it.

Voice is coherent; tone adapts to consequence and context. Health-sensitive review
may need calm uncertainty, not cheerleading or judgment. Routine success can be
brief. Avoid generic enthusiasm, repeated helper prose, patronizing encouragement,
or apologetic/playful templates that conflict with the accepted identity.

Prefer concrete verbs and useful nouns; preserve domain terms users know. Check
long content and localization when wording affects layout, and align visible and
accessible names. Use existing terminology/voice documentation; a short glossary
is warranted only for recurring inconsistency. Broad renaming or a new product
promise returns to the scope gate. Do not create campaign or persona artifacts
for a local wording correction.
